# Literature Review: Mapping the Agave Boom in Oaxaca's DO Mezcal Region (2015–2025)

*Compiled by the literature-review agent for the `agave-boom-oaxaca` project, 2026-05-09. Citations follow author-year style; full BibTeX entries (matching the keys used here) are in `paper/bibliography/refs.bib`.*

---

## 1. Introduction

The decade between 2015 and 2025 has brought an extraordinary transformation to the rural landscape of Oaxaca, Mexico. Mezcal, once a regional spirit produced almost entirely for local consumption, has become a globally traded luxury commodity, with annual exports rising from roughly one million liters in the mid-2010s to upward of eight million liters in 2024 (Consejo Regulador del Mezcal 2024). The agronomic substrate of that boom — *Agave* spp., predominantly *A. angustifolia* "espadín" — has expanded in cultivated area at rates reported in case studies to exceed 400% over a thirty-year window in the most active producing valleys (Lira et al. 2022). This literature review situates a forthcoming study that aims to be the first wall-to-wall, decadal, peer-review-ready geospatial reconstruction of agave expansion across the Oaxacan portion of the Mezcal Denominación de Origen (DO Mezcal).

The contribution we propose sits at the intersection of three thus-far weakly connected literatures: (i) the Mexican rural-sociology and food-systems scholarship on the political economy of mezcal (Bowen 2015; Tetreault et al. 2021; Robson et al. 2022); (ii) operational remote-sensing methods for perennial crop mapping at 10 m resolution from Sentinel-1/2 time series (Belgiu and Drăguț 2016; Veloso et al. 2017; Pelletier et al. 2019; Sainte Fare Garnot et al. 2020); and (iii) the methodological canon for area-adjusted, design-based accuracy assessment in land-change studies (Olofsson et al. 2013, 2014; Stehman and Foody 2019; GOFC-GOLD 2016). The remainder of this review traverses these literatures cluster by cluster and ends with an explicit gap statement that motivates the present project.

## 2. Mezcal and the Agave Boom: Industry, Policy, and Social Context

Bowen's *Divided Spirits* (Bowen 2015) remains the canonical English-language analysis of how the Mexican Denominaciones de Origen for tequila and mezcal have, paradoxically, accelerated the commodification and consolidation of what were historically smallholder, biodiverse spirits industries. Bowen documents how the legal architecture of the DO — protected by NOM-070 in the case of mezcal — produces inclusions and exclusions that reshape which communities, agave species, and practices are legible as "authentic," and which are excluded from market premiums. Valenzuela-Zapata and Nabhan (2003) provide the deepest historical and ethnobotanical treatment of agave spirits in west-central Mexico, including the agronomic decisions that converted the Jalisco tequila belt from a polycultural to a monocultural blue-agave landscape; Valenzuela-Zapata et al. (2011) extend that argument to the boom–bust dynamics of agave prices, arguing that the cyclic behavior of the tequila industry foreshadows risks now appearing in mezcal. Tetreault et al. (2021), writing in the *Journal of Agrarian Change*, advance the concept of "agro-extractivism" as an analytic lens for the industrial concentration of agave-tequila value chains and the rental of ejidal land by transnational distillers, an analysis whose central mechanisms are increasingly visible in Oaxaca.

Specific to Oaxaca, the most directly relevant peer-reviewed study to date is Lira et al. (2022) in *Land*, which uses a community-scale case study to document a stepwise expansion of agave cropland from 6% to 22% of the local land base across three market epochs (1993–2001, 2001–2013, 2013–2019) and identifies tropical dry forest as the principal class displaced. Robson et al. (2022) in *Agriculture and Human Values* document the institutional dynamics — communal property regimes, customary governance, and inter-community organizations such as the Buin Dannis Union of Communities and Ejidos — that mediate between global mezcal demand and local land-use outcomes. Hernández-Hernández and Acosta-Díaz (2022) and Vásquez-García et al. (2021) provide complementary value-chain and territorial-development perspectives. The ethnobotanical and agroecological literature on Oaxacan agaves — Aguirre-Rivera et al. (2001), Vargas-Ponce et al. (2007), Larson et al. (2010), Delgado-Lemus et al. (2014), Rangel-Landa et al. (2016), and Torres-García et al. (2019) — establishes both the diversity of managed and wild *Agave* species in the Oaxaca-Puebla-Tehuacán region and the fragility of that diversity in the face of monoculture pressure. García-Mendoza et al. (2025) synthesize emerging "rewilding" practices within the mezcal market, while Guevara-Gutiérrez et al. (2024) provide quantitative evidence that intensive blue-agave management drives soil erosion under the row-and-furrow regime now diffusing into Oaxaca. Producer-association statistics from the Consejo Regulador del Mezcal (2024) and the legal framing of the DO (DOF 2018) provide the institutional and quantitative backdrop. Across this cluster, the central empirical claim is consistent: a rapid, market-led, geographically uneven expansion is under way, but no peer-reviewed study has yet quantified that expansion at fine spatial resolution across the entire DO Mezcal Oaxaca region.

## 3. Remote Sensing of Agave and Analog Perennial Crops

The remote-sensing literature on agave specifically is sparse and almost entirely focused on the Jalisco tequila belt and on small geographic windows. Velasco-Ramírez et al. (2017), in *Terra Latinoamericana*, present an early study using Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery to detect production restrictions in *Agave tequilana* var. azul plantations in the municipalities of Tequila, Amatitán, and El Arenal; their work establishes that agave's distinctive spectral signature is detectable at 10 m resolution but does not address landscape-scale expansion or rigorous accuracy assessment. UAV-based plant-counting and row-detection studies (Calvario et al. 2020; Flores et al. 2021; Villatoro et al. 2024; Ramírez-García et al. 2025) have produced high-accuracy plant-level inventories using mathematical morphology and deep learning, but their spatial coverage is field-scale rather than regional. Most recently, Escárcega-Castillo et al. (2023) demonstrated agave segmentation and maturity classification with deep learning on very-high-resolution satellite imagery, again limited to demonstration windows. Crucially, no published study applies satellite-based agave detection to a multi-year, regional, area-adjusted accuracy framework — and none focus on Oaxaca or on *A. angustifolia* (espadín), whose canopy architecture differs from the more compact rosette of blue agave. This is a foundational gap that the present project addresses directly.

In the absence of direct precedent, methodological transferability is sought from the broader perennial / row-crop remote-sensing literature. Vuolo et al. (2018) and Griffiths et al. (2019) demonstrate that intra-annual Sentinel-2 reflectance composites materially improve crop-type classification accuracy at 10–20 m, with multi-temporal information typically dominating spectral information for distinguishing crops with similar spectral but distinct phenological signatures. Veloso et al. (2017) and Nasrallah et al. (2019) establish that Sentinel-1 SAR backscatter and the VV/VH ratio are sensitive to canopy structure and seasonal phenology, providing complementary information to optical sensors and partly bypassing cloud-cover problems that are severe in Sierra Sur and Sierra Norte during the rainy season. Hauser et al. (2024) and Somers et al. (2023) further show that combined optical-SAR time series with phenology metrics enable accurate national-scale tree-species and orchard / vineyard / olive-grove delineation — directly analogous problems to mapping a long-cycle, perennial, structurally distinctive crop such as agave embedded in a heterogeneous matrix. These studies collectively argue for a feature-stack approach (annual percentile composites, harmonic-regression coefficients, GLCM texture, and S1 backscatter) that we adopt in our methods.

## 4. Methods Foundations: Classification, Time Series, and Validation

The classification side of our pipeline rests on two main strands. Random-forest classification (Breiman 2001) is reviewed comprehensively for remote-sensing applications by Belgiu and Drăguț (2016), who report that random forest reliably handles high-dimensional, multicollinear feature stacks, exhibits competitive accuracy across application domains, and provides interpretable variable-importance metrics — properties we exploit both for prediction and for diagnostic feature ranking. The deep-learning strand is dominated by temporal-convolutional and attention architectures: Pelletier et al. (2019) introduced TempCNN as a 1-D temporal convolutional network that outperforms random forests and recurrent networks on satellite image time series; Sainte Fare Garnot et al. (2020) extended this with the Pixel-Set Encoder + Temporal Attention Encoder (PSE+TAE), and the lightweight L-TAE variant (Sainte Fare Garnot and Landrieu 2020) reduces compute cost while preserving accuracy. We treat random forest as our baseline and PSE+TAE / TempCNN as stretch-goal architectures for years where the dense Sentinel-2 record is available.

For change detection and trajectory smoothing we draw on three lineages. LandTrendr (Kennedy et al. 2010) provides temporal segmentation of yearly Landsat trajectories into monotonic segments, useful for identifying establishment-year breakpoints that mark agave planting events. BFAST (Verbesselt et al. 2010) decomposes time series into seasonal, trend, and residual components and detects breaks within each, offering an alternative formulation appropriate when sub-annual phenological signals are informative. The Continuous Change Detection and Classification (CCDC) framework (Zhu and Woodcock 2014) generalizes both approaches to a fully continuous, all-available-data setting. Hansen et al. (2013) and Brown et al. (2022) — *Global Forest Change* and *Dynamic World* — supply globally consistent baseline products that we use both as covariates and as cross-validation references at coarse aggregation. Wulder et al. (2019) document the analysis-ready Landsat archive that bridges our 2015–2017 pre-Sentinel-2 period; Gorelick et al. (2017) describe the Earth Engine platform on which most of our compute occurs; Funk et al. (2015) provide the CHIRPS precipitation record we use as a climatic covariate.

Our validation strategy follows the now-canonical Olofsson family of papers. Olofsson et al. (2013) demonstrated how a classification confusion matrix obtained from a stratified probability sample yields unbiased area estimates with quantified uncertainty, and Olofsson et al. (2014) consolidated those recommendations into the field-standard "good practices" template covering sampling design, response design, and analysis. Stehman and Foody (2019) update this guidance with attention to imperfect reference data and design-vs-model-based inference, and the GOFC-GOLD (2016) sourcebook provides the operational reference for monitoring frameworks linked to UNFCCC reporting. We adopt a stratified probability sample over the final classified map, report user's, producer's, and overall accuracy with confidence intervals, and report area-adjusted estimates. Mitchell et al. (2019) provide the Model Cards framework that we use to document the trained classifier with disclosures spanning intended use, evaluation factors, and ethical considerations.

## 5. Land-Use / Land-Cover Change in Oaxaca and Mexico

Mexican LULC scholarship offers both mapping products and a problem framing. Velázquez et al. (2002) catalog national-scale rates and patterns of land-cover change derived from INEGI Series II–III, and Velázquez et al. (2003) drill into Oaxaca specifically, reporting that highly biodiverse landscapes there have been simultaneously losing forest in some regions and recovering in others, with strong heterogeneity by tenure regime. Trejo and Dirzo (2000) document the disproportionate vulnerability of seasonally dry tropical forest — the dominant non-forest matrix of the Valles Centrales and Sierra Sur where most espadín expansion is occurring. Bonilla-Moheno and Aide (2020) provide a national-scale synthesis of post-NAFTA Mexican land transitions ("beyond deforestation"), showing that the dominant signal in many regions is not net forest loss but a churn of crops, pasture, and secondary vegetation; Bonilla-Moheno et al. (2016) zoom into southern Oaxaca's tropical dry forest to identify rainfed agriculture and pastureland as the leading proximate causes of forest loss between 1985 and 2006.

The MAD-Mex system (Gebhardt et al. 2014) operationalizes wall-to-wall Landsat-based land-cover mapping for Mexico's REDD-MRV program; in combination with INEGI's vector Series VI (2016) and VII (2021) it provides the official baseline against which our classification can be cross-tabulated. Bray et al. (2006) and Barsimantov and Antezana (2012) establish that tenure regime — communal vs. individualized ejidos vs. private parcels — is a first-order predictor of forest cover dynamics in Mexico, with community-managed ejidos consistently exhibiting lower deforestation than parcelized holdings; Ellis et al. (2020) link 1992 agrarian counter-reforms to shifts in landscape structure across Yucatán. These tenure dynamics are directly relevant to Oaxaca, where 80% of forested land is under social tenure (ejido or comunidad agraria) and where the coexistence of customary and statutory governance shapes the geography of the boom (Robson et al. 2022). Although none of these papers focus specifically on agave, together they (i) bound the credible space of LULC transitions, (ii) provide cross-validation products, and (iii) supply tenure and demographic covariates for our driver model.

## 6. Spatial Statistics for Hotspots and Trends

Identifying *where* and *when* expansion is concentrated requires the standard toolkit of spatial statistics. Anselin (1995) introduced Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA), enabling decomposition of global Moran's I into observation-level contributions and the identification of local clusters and outliers. Getis and Ord (1992) developed the Gi and Gi* statistics for detecting hot and cold spots within a specified neighborhood, and Cliff and Ord (1981) provide the foundational treatment of spatial autocorrelation models that underwrites both. We apply LISA and Gi* to municipal-level expansion-rate surfaces, using row-standardized contiguity-based and inverse-distance weights. For the temporal dimension, the nonparametric Mann-Kendall trend test (Mann 1945; Kendall 1975) identifies monotonic trends in annual area without distributional assumptions, and the Sen slope (Sen 1968) provides a robust median-based magnitude estimator. These tests are well-suited to the short, possibly autocorrelated annual time series we will produce and tolerate the heteroscedasticity expected in early-period (Landsat-bridged) and late-period (Sentinel-2) estimates.

## 7. CAM Physiology and the Spectral Signature of Agave

The remote-sensing legibility of agave plantations rests fundamentally on the physiology of CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) photosynthesis. Nobel's foundational work — beginning with the canonical 1976 study on water relations and photosynthesis in *Agave deserti* (Nobel 1976) and synthesized in his book-length treatment (Nobel 2010) — establishes that obligate CAM species fix CO₂ at night, maintain closed stomata during the day, and consequently maintain green canopy and high water content even through prolonged drought. Borland et al. (2009) extend the argument to bioenergy, showing that CAM crops achieve approximately four to six times the water-use efficiency of C₄ and C₃ crops respectively. Stewart (2015) explicitly frames agave as a model CAM crop for warming and drying climates, and Davis et al. (2017) and Owen et al. (2016) provide eddy-covariance and field-trial confirmation that agave canopy CO₂ exchange, water flux, and biomass accumulation are tightly coupled to the four-phase CAM gas-exchange signature.

For our purposes, three implications follow. First, CAM canopy NDVI / EVI is high and remarkably stable across the dry season, a signature that contrasts sharply with milpa and other rainfed annual crops whose canopies senesce after harvest in November-December. Second, the inverted day-night gas-exchange phase of CAM means that water-stress-driven NDVI dips on annual scales are smaller in agave than in C₃ / C₄ neighbors, producing a diagnostic seasonal-amplitude signature in harmonic-regression coefficients. Third, the long establishment-to-harvest cycle of espadín (typically 6–8 years) produces a multi-year monotonic NDVI rise after planting that is recoverable with LandTrendr-style segmentation, providing a temporal fingerprint distinct from short-cycle annuals. These physiological features are why, despite the absence of a direct agave-mapping precedent at our scale, we can credibly expect 10 m Sentinel-2-based detection to perform well — provided phenology metrics, not single-date spectra, are the basis of classification.

## 8. Drivers of Commodity-Driven LULC Change

To frame the agave boom as part of the broader political economy of commodity-driven land-system change, we draw on the global synthesis literature. Foley et al. (2005), in *Science*, articulated the now-canonical framing of land-use change as a global driver of biophysical and biogeochemical transformation. Curtis et al. (2018), again in *Science*, classified global tree-cover loss into five proximate drivers and attributed 27% of global forest loss to permanent land-use change for commodity production, with regionally distinct signatures of palm oil, soy, cattle, and shifting agriculture. Pendrill et al. (2019a, b) quantify the share of tropical deforestation embodied in international trade — 29–39% by their estimates — and identify the displacement dynamics by which boom commodities diffuse spatially. Lambin and Meyfroidt (2011) provide the conceptual backbone for understanding such displacement under conditions of economic globalization. Although none of these global studies single out agave or mezcal — neither appears in Curtis et al.'s classification — the analytic frame they provide is directly applicable: agave for export-oriented mezcal is, by every operational definition, a commodity-driven LULC transition; tracking it at fine spatial resolution allows us to test whether the global pattern documented for palm, soy, and beef is reproduced at smaller scale by an emerging boom commodity. Tetreault et al. (2021) and Lira et al. (2022) anticipate this framing in the Mexican literature; our project provides the geospatial evidence base.

## 9. Synthesis and Gap Statement

Taken together, the literature establishes three uncontested points: (i) the mezcal boom is real, rapid, and concentrated in Oaxaca (Bowen 2015; Robson et al. 2022; Lira et al. 2022; CRT 2024); (ii) the methodological toolbox to detect, validate, and analyze such expansion at 10 m resolution is mature, though it has not yet been assembled for agave (Olofsson et al. 2014; Belgiu and Drăguț 2016; Pelletier et al. 2019; Brown et al. 2022); and (iii) the physiological basis for spectral-temporal detection of agave is well-understood (Nobel 1976, 2010; Stewart 2015), but to our knowledge has never been operationalized as a phenology-feature-based classifier at regional scale.

What is missing, and what this project supplies, is a **systematic, decadal, wall-to-wall, area-adjusted, peer-review-ready geospatial reconstruction of agave expansion across the entire Oaxacan portion of the Mezcal DO**. Existing remote-sensing studies of agave are confined to Jalisco and to small Oaxacan windows, never use stratified probability sampling for area-adjusted estimation in the Olofsson tradition, and do not produce annual time series of agave area at a cadence that supports trend testing or driver modeling. Existing socioeconomic studies of the mezcal boom rely on partial industry statistics (CRT, SIAP) that are widely acknowledged to under-report planted area. Existing Mexican LULC products (MAD-Mex, INEGI Series VI/VII, Hansen GFC, Dynamic World) classify forest, agriculture, and bare land, but none distinguish agave from other crops at 10 m resolution.

The forthcoming project closes this gap by integrating (a) Sentinel-2 / Sentinel-1 / Landsat feature stacks; (b) random-forest classification supplemented by deep temporal architectures; (c) Hidden Markov / LandTrendr post-processing for trajectory consistency; (d) Olofsson-compliant stratified accuracy assessment; (e) LISA / Gi* hotspot analysis and Mann-Kendall / Sen-slope trend testing; (f) panel regression of municipal expansion against export volumes, palenque density, road access, and prior land cover; and (g) cross-tabulation against INEGI Series VI / VII to characterize displaced land covers. The resulting dataset will be the first peer-reviewable geospatial record of the agave boom in Oaxaca and a methodological template transferable to other emerging boom commodities in semi-arid Latin America.
